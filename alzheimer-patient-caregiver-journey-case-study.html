Mapping Alzheimer's patient and caregiver journeys.

The patient journey changes the caregiver's life, too. This study connected online accounts, information searches, and support conversations to show what families needed at each stage.

Research question

What do families need beyond information about the condition?

The original study examined how people discussed Alzheimer's disease online in Brazil. Its objective was to organize perceptions and experiences around symptoms, diagnosis, treatment, and consequences for quality of life, including the impact on caregivers and family members.

A central finding was that the online patient journey was often narrated by someone else. Family caregivers asked for specialist recommendations, explained changes in routines, discussed medication questions, and looked for help with the emotional and practical work of care.

Understanding that voice matters. A caregiver's account can reveal unmet support needs and difficult handoffs, but it is not interchangeable with a first-person patient interview. The analysis kept the speaker and the person receiving care conceptually distinct.

Core insight

Awareness of the condition did not answer the next practical question.

Families still needed to know whom to contact, how to reorganize care, and where to get support when daily life changed.

Methodology

Digital ethnography with a journey framework.

The method connected the language of online conversations with the decisions and support needs described in the research report.

01 · Source discovery

Find the conversation.

The original method used keyword-based discovery of online publications related to Alzheimer's disease. Sources included Facebook, YouTube, Instagram, and Twitter, alongside search-interest and traditional media analysis.

02 · Contextual interpretation

Identify who is speaking.

The report distinguished caregivers, relatives, patients, professionals, associations, media, and the wider public. That distinction helped separate firsthand care accounts from general awareness and entertainment content.

03 · Journey synthesis

Connect accounts to stages.

Experiences were organized around noticing changes, seeking a diagnosis, managing care, and dealing with the consequences. The journey presentation captured barriers, information channels, changes in routines, and questions for professionals.

04 · Opportunity interpretation

Turn recurring needs into a brief.

The synthesis identified places where clearer navigation, practical guidance, caregiver recognition, and support could matter. These are research implications, not evidence that an intervention was implemented or improved outcomes.

This portfolio version draws on the archived Alzheimer's netnography report and patient journey presentation. It does not add interviews, participant counts, or an analysis of current social media activity.

Visual journey map

The information need changes as the work of care changes.

Five analytical stages summarize the original report. They organize the evidence; they are not a fixed clinical progression or a timeline every family follows.

  2. 02Seek assessmentFind appropriate support
  3. 03Reorganize careShare responsibilities
  4. 04Manage daily lifeAdapt routines and support
  5. 05Consider added supportReassess care arrangements
01

Notice changes

Patient experience as reported

Relatives described changes in memory, orientation, communication, or behavior that they did not yet know how to interpret.

Caregiver work

Notice patterns, search for an explanation, and decide when to discuss the concern with the family.

Information need

“Which changes should we discuss with a professional?”

Observed friction

Uncertainty about aging, other conditions, and where to start.

02

Seek assessment

Patient experience as reported

The report included accounts of consultations, diagnostic uncertainty, and delays in recognizing what the changes meant.

Caregiver work

Look for specialists, ask about appointments and tests, and make sense of information from different sources.

Information need

“Who should we contact, and what happens next?”

Observed friction

Difficulty finding specialist support and understanding the assessment process.

03

Reorganize care

Patient experience as reported

Families described changes in living arrangements, daily routines, and communication after a diagnosis.

Caregiver work

Discuss responsibilities, coordinate support, and help the family understand the practical implications.

Information need

“How do we organize care without leaving one person to do everything?”

Observed friction

Uneven family participation, emotional strain, and uncertainty about the new routine.

04

Manage daily life

Patient experience as reported

Care accounts described changing needs around communication, personal care, medication routines, and day-to-day activities.

Caregiver work

Adapt routines, ask practical questions, seek professional guidance, and find a way to rest.

Information need

“How do we handle today's difficulty, and who can help?”

Observed friction

Exhaustion, limited support, uncertainty about medication concerns, and difficulty sustaining routines.

05

Consider added support

Patient experience as reported

The archive described increasing dependence and consideration of different care arrangements.

Caregiver work

Compare professional or residential support, discuss costs and responsibilities, and seek reassurance about the decision.

Information need

“What additional support is appropriate for our family?”

Observed friction

Financial pressure, guilt, and the fear that seeking outside care could be interpreted as abandonment.

Questions are editorial summaries of recurring information needs, not verbatim participant quotations. Rows synthesize the historical report and journey map; no clinical recommendations are implied.

Information and support ecosystem

Different channels served different jobs.

The study described an information journey across search, communities, professional explanations, and family stories. The channels below reflect the historical material, not current platform reach.

At the center

Patient and caregiver needs

Understand · Find help · Coordinate · Cope

Search

Find explanations, specialists, appointment information, and answers to emerging questions.

Facebook communities

Ask practical questions, exchange experiences, request recommendations, and talk about the burden of care.

YouTube

Watch professional explanations and care routines, with comments adding questions and personal accounts.

Instagram and Twitter

Share family stories, awareness content, and public discussion. Visibility alone did not indicate a direct care need.

Associations connected awareness with support.

The report described associations as sources of education, volunteer networks, advocacy, and help for families. Their role suggests an important distinction for a support journey: receiving information and finding a usable next step are separate needs.

Findings

Caregiver support is part of the patient experience.

01 · Voice and perspective

The visible voice was often the caregiver's.

The report found that patient experiences were frequently shared by relatives. A patient-only communication strategy could miss the person searching for information, organizing care, and making arrangements.

Implication: distinguish patient needs from caregiver tasks in recruitment, content, and service design.

02 · Access and navigation

Families needed a route, not just awareness.

Questions about specialists, tests, and next steps appeared alongside general information about the condition. Recognizing a concern did not necessarily make the care pathway clear.

Implication: investigate how families move from an explanation to an appropriate source of professional help.

03 · Practical and emotional work

Daily care carried a second workload.

Caregivers discussed exhaustion, limited family support, communication difficulties, and changes in routines. Emotional support and practical assistance appeared together, rather than as separate stages.

Implication: treat caregiver capacity and support as part of a service research brief.

04 · Additional care

Seeking outside support was also an identity decision.

The archive described guilt and fear of abandonment when families considered residential care or professional support. Information about options alone did not address that emotional context.

Implication: test how support options are explained, while respecting the family's circumstances and choices.

How the evidence can be used

Turn the journey into a focused product or service brief.

Patient support programs

Organize information around the family's next question. Specify when professional navigation, peer support, and practical care resources need to connect.

Digital health experiences

Create a research brief for caregiver onboarding, role recognition, findability, and comprehension. Test it with the intended users before claiming an improvement.

Communication and education

Match the message to the stage and the speaker. Separate general awareness from the practical needs of someone managing care that day.

These are applications of the findings. The archive does not establish that these interventions were launched or that they changed access, caregiver burden, or clinical outcomes.

Evidence and limitations

A historical, exploratory view of the journey.

The source report uses qualitative online material and search/media analysis. Public discourse is shaped by platform visibility, who chooses to post, and which conversations can be collected. It cannot establish population prevalence, verify a poster's diagnosis, or represent every patient and caregiver.

The online voices in the archive include caregivers describing someone else's experience. Those accounts are useful for understanding support needs, but they do not replace direct patient research. Current questions should be tested through a separately scoped study.

This portfolio version summarizes findings without names, handles, identifiable images, or verbatim posts. Clinical claims and historical treatment discussions are excluded from the case's recommendations.

Source basis: the archived Alzheimer's netnography report, containing material through 2022, and its patient journey presentation. The English journey visualization is an adaptation of those materials. The collection window varies by source; this page does not assert a single exact sampling period.
