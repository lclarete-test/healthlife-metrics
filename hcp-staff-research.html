Featured HCP Research

Digital profiling for an oncology medical device launch.

See how professional networks, information sources and qualitative clinician profiles were combined to support audience segmentation for a medical device launch in breast cancer care.

The challenge

Build a launch around the clinicians it needs to reach.

A medical device launch needed a clearer view of the clinicians connected to breast cancer surgery: which professional communities they belonged to, what information they consumed and how they discussed surgical decisions.

The research combined digital profiling, social network analysis and qualitative review to turn public digital signals into an audience segmentation and a more specific communication brief.

What we delivered

Audience evidence for a sharper launch brief.

  • Professional network mapAffinity clusters and influential accounts.
  • Information-source profileJournals, institutions, media and events.
  • Clinician profiles and segmentationPublic clinical viewpoints and communication priorities.

Professional network analysis

Map the clinical communities around the device.

The network analysis identified four connected communities: breast surgeons, plastic surgeons, specialists in plastic reconstruction and general medicine. Their connections placed breast cancer care within a wider professional conversation that also included reconstruction, patient outcomes and clinical practice.

We used this map to organize the audience before looking more closely at individual clinicians. The qualitative review then added context from their publications, interviews and public discussions, connecting professional relationships with the clinical questions they were discussing.

Launch implications

Start with the clinical context behind each audience.

A broad label such as “oncology clinicians” does not explain which conversations a device needs to enter. The network brought several relevant professional communities into view, giving the research a more specific starting point.

The clinician review explored three practical questions:

  • Clinical priorities: What did clinicians discuss about surgical margins, re-excision and patient outcomes?
  • Professional influence: Which societies, institutions and clinician accounts appeared in their networks?
  • Information needs: How did they discuss clinical evidence, new technology and existing guidelines?

Together, the map and profile review provided a basis for audience-specific communication briefs.

HCP audience insights

Scientific relevance belongs at the center of the message.

Health and fitness, and science, were the strongest interests shown in the audience profile. Both were substantially higher than the platform’s general US benchmark. Technology and computing also appeared, adding context to a study focused on clinicians’ views of new surgical tools.

These findings helped frame the next research question: what kind of evidence would make a new device clinically relevant to this audience? The qualitative review explored that question through discussions of surgical decisions, margin assessment, patient experience and new technology.

Launch implications

Connect the technology to a clinical question.

The health and science profile supported an evidence-led approach to clinician communication. A useful launch brief would connect device features to questions clinicians already raised: assessing margins, making decisions during surgery and considering outcomes that matter to patients.

That creates concrete directions for content development: summaries of relevant research, explanations of how a technology fits into the surgical workflow, and clinical scenarios that address a specific decision.

The audience data helps identify these directions. The clinician profiles supply the context needed to make the content specific.

HCP influence and information sources

Find the people and sources connected to the audience.

The influence analysis brought together professional societies, individual clinicians, research publications and clinical institutions. The American Society of Breast Surgeons, the American College of Surgeons, the Society of Surgical Oncology and ASCO featured in the profile, alongside clinicians such as Deanna Attai and Judy Boughey.

For medical device launch planning, this is a practical information map. It identifies sources to review for clinical language, evidence discussions and emerging questions. It also helps distinguish the role of institutions from the role of individual clinicians in the professional conversation.

HCP media affinities

Understand the wider information environment.

The audience profile extended beyond medical journals and professional societies. Health news, national newspapers, radio and digital publications also appeared, including NBC News Health, NPR Health News, WebMD and major news outlets.

This matters for content strategy because clinical audiences encounter information in several formats. Scientific detail, a clinician interview and a plain-language explanation serve different purposes. The media analysis gave the launch team a broader set of formats and sources to consider when developing its communication plan.

HCP segmentation

Separate professional context from clinical priorities.

The professional segmentation covered three contexts: major clinical centers, clinicians with a strong academic publication record, and small or midsize hospitals. This structure helped organize the profile review around the different environments in which clinicians worked and shared knowledge.

We then examined their public clinical viewpoints. The questions were specific: how did they discuss surgical margins, what role did new technology play in their work, and how did they balance guidelines with patient-specific decisions? This added a behavioral layer to the professional profile.

Major clinical centers

Clinicians working at large institutions such as Mayo Clinic. Their profiles provided context on specialist practice, research activity and the educational content shared by major centers.

Academic contributors

Clinicians with a substantial publication record. Reviewing their papers and public commentary helped connect clinical viewpoints to the research and evidence they discussed.

Small and midsize hospitals

Clinicians in smaller institutional settings. Including this context broadened the review beyond prominent research centers and kept the segmentation attentive to different practice environments.

These are professional lenses, not mutually exclusive groups or measured population shares.

Three qualitative personas emerged from the research.

Tech-driven effectiveness

Evidence and new tools.

This persona centered on clinical effectiveness, research and technologies that support decisions during surgery. The profile emphasized margin assessment and reducing the need for additional procedures.

Communication direction: explain the evidence, intended use and role of the device within the surgical workflow.

The “just right” approach

Patient-specific decisions.

This persona emphasized individualized treatment, tissue preservation and the balance between disease control and patient experience. Public discussions included oncoplastic approaches and decisions tailored to each case.

Communication direction: connect the technology to patient-specific clinical scenarios and the outcomes clinicians are trying to balance.

Strict-guidelines

Confidence in established practice.

This persona emphasized safety, established guidelines and practical clarity. It brought implementation questions into the segmentation: how a device fits existing routines and what clinicians need to understand before changing practice.

Communication direction: provide clear evidence, practical education and a concrete explanation of workflow fit.

Qualitative synthesis from the original persona report. The Strict-guidelines persona was informed partly by other clinicians’ accounts.

Audience profile

Use demographics to describe the sample.

The demographic and location estimates added context to the professional network. They helped describe the analyzed audience, while the clinician review supplied the clinical detail needed for segmentation. The two sources served different purposes within the study.

These charts are platform-derived estimates for the sampled social audience. They are useful for interpreting this profile and should not be treated as a census of US oncologists or as verified individual clinician demographics.

Research methodology

Combine network data with a closer look at clinical practice.

The study connected quantitative digital audience analysis with qualitative review of publicly available clinician material. Network and affinity data described the audience at a group level. Publications, interviews and public discussions added the detail needed to build clinician profiles and interpret differences in clinical priorities.

Sources

Public Twitter profiles, following and follower relationships, clinician posts, published research, interviews, congress material, and content from clinical institutions and professional organizations. Twitter is now known as X.

Methodology

Profile discovery combined automated collection with manual assessment of clinician relevance. Audiense supported quantitative audience profiling and affinity analysis. A qualitative review examined 16 physicians’ public digital footprints, and the research report included 14 detailed clinician profiles. Findings were organized into professional contexts and qualitative personas to support launch communication planning.

Scope and interpretation

This historical study focused on a breast-care clinician audience. Affinities represent platform-estimated connections or interests, rather than verified consumption, endorsement or adoption. Professional contexts may overlap, and the qualitative personas do not represent measured population shares. The deliverables supported audience understanding and launch planning; campaign performance and commercial launch outcomes were outside this case study.
